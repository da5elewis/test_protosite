Prompt Engineering for Strategy Briefs

Can an LLM turn bullet-point notes into a client-ready strategy brief? We tested three approaches — and the differences were significant.

Context & Goal

After a client workshop, we're often left with a wall of sticky notes, a messy Miro board, and a brief to write by morning. The question: can an LLM do the heavy lifting of turning raw, unstructured notes into a coherent brief — well enough to be a real starting point rather than a toy?

The goal wasn't to see if AI could replace the thinking. It was to see if it could compress the time between "raw notes" and "structured first draft" — which is often where momentum dies.

What We Tried

We tested three prompting approaches using the same set of raw notes from a real (anonymized) strategy workshop:

Approach A — Zero context: Simply pasted the notes and asked Claude to "write a strategy brief from these." No role, no format, no guidance.

Approach B — Role prompting: Set context upfront: "You are a senior strategy consultant. Your client is a mid-market financial services firm. Write a one-page strategy brief from these workshop notes aimed at a VP-level audience." Same notes, same model.

Approach C — Few-shot with template: Provided a brief example of a well-structured strategy brief (with placeholder content), then asked Claude to use that format and style with the actual notes. Role context included.

Results

Approach A produced a generic, listy document. It captured the content but missed the logic — there was no throughline, no recommended direction, no audience awareness. It read like a summary, not a strategy brief.

Approach B was markedly better. Having a defined role and audience caused Claude to make choices — it elevated some points, buried others, and organized around a central argument. It wasn't perfect, but it was genuinely useful as a starting point.

Approach C was the strongest output. Providing a structural example meant Claude adopted a consistent format, made appropriate section choices, and matched the tone expected by a VP audience. The output needed editing, not rewriting.

Key Result

Moving from Approach A to Approach C reduced editing time from ~90 minutes to ~25 minutes in our estimate. The content quality jumped most noticeably in logical structure and audience calibration.

Complications & Caveats

The few-shot example introduced a risk: the model sometimes leaned too heavily on the example's structure even when the content didn't call for it. We had to push back in a follow-up to restructure one section.

Also worth noting: the more specific the role prompt, the more important it is that the role accurately reflects the real context. A mismatch between the stated role ("senior consultant for a Fortune 500 tech firm") and the actual client situation led to off-key language that took time to undo.

Takeaway
The Principle

The gap between a useless AI output and a useful one is almost entirely determined by the context you set at the start. Role + audience + format example is your minimum viable prompt for structured deliverables.

The implication for team workflows: build a small library of prompt templates with role definitions and example outputs for the deliverable types you produce repeatedly — briefs, POVs, workshop reports, recommendation decks. The template becomes the institutional memory, not just the individual.
